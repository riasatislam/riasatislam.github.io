Our preprint, **Designing for Interconnected Islamic Learning: A Qualitative Study of Muslim Women's Experiences with Qur'an, Hadith, and Seerah Apps**, is now available online.

The paper is currently available as an **arXiv preprint** and is under review with a reputed journal. That means the work is public and readable, but it should still be treated as a preprint until peer review is complete.

You can read the publication page here: [Designing for Interconnected Islamic Learning](/publication/easha-interconnected-islamic-learning-2026/).

## Why interconnected Islamic learning matters

Islamic learning is rarely isolated.

When people read the Qur'an, they often want to understand context, language, tafsir, related hadith, Seerah background, and practical lessons. When they read a hadith, they may want to know where it sits within wider Islamic teaching. When they study the Seerah, they may want to connect events to revelation, character, worship, and everyday life.

But many digital tools separate these learning pathways.

One app may focus on Qur'an reading. Another may focus on Hadith. Another may provide Seerah material. Each app may be useful, but the learner has to do the work of connecting them. They move between screens, search boxes, translations, bookmarks, and notes, often without a clear sense of how the pieces fit together.

Our study looks at this problem from a human-computer interaction perspective. It asks: how do Muslim users actually move between Qur'an, Hadith, and Seerah apps, and what kinds of design support would make Islamic learning more connected without making it overwhelming?

## What the study explored

The paper is based on five semi-structured interviews with Muslim women who use Islamic apps for learning and reflection.

The aim was not to measure app popularity or compare feature lists. The aim was to understand lived experience: how people search, read, cross-check, reflect, and decide whether an Islamic learning tool feels useful and trustworthy.

Several recurring tensions appeared in the interviews:

- users want more context, but not clutter
- users value guidance, but do not want the app to overstep
- users want connected sources, but still need clear provenance
- users want flexibility across quick reading and deeper study
- users want authenticity without losing devotional flow

These tensions matter because Islamic learning apps are not just information products. They shape how people encounter sacred knowledge, build habits, ask questions, and develop confidence.

## Layered contextuality

One of the key ideas in the paper is **layered contextuality**.

By layered contextuality, we mean that Islamic learning tools should provide context in layers rather than all at once.

A user reading a Qur'anic verse may first want a simple translation. Then they may want a short explanation. Then they may want related hadith. Then they may want Seerah background, scholarly commentary, or practical application. Another user may only want the first layer at that moment.

The design challenge is to make this expansion trustworthy, optional, and respectful of the user's purpose.

Good contextual design should not flood the screen. It should not turn every moment of reading into a maze of links. It should help users move deeper when they are ready, while preserving clarity for those who want a focused devotional experience.

This is especially important on mobile devices, where attention, screen space, and time are limited.

## Trust is a design requirement

In Islamic apps, trust is not a nice extra. It is a core requirement.

Users need to know where content comes from. They need to distinguish between Qur'anic text, translation, hadith, commentary, summary, and app-generated guidance. They need confidence that the app is not mixing sources carelessly or presenting interpretation as if it were direct revelation.

This becomes even more important as Islamic apps begin to use AI, semantic search, recommendation systems, and generated summaries.

The more powerful the tool becomes, the more important provenance becomes.

A useful Islamic learning app should answer questions such as:

- What source is this content from?
- Is this a translation, commentary, summary, or generated explanation?
- Can I inspect the original reference?
- Is the app helping me understand, or pushing me into more content?
- Does the design preserve adab with sacred knowledge?

These are not only theological questions. They are design questions.

## Devotional flow and study modes

Another important theme is that users approach Islamic learning in different modes.

Sometimes a person wants a quick reminder. Sometimes they want to read after prayer. Sometimes they want to study seriously, compare sources, take notes, or prepare for teaching. A single app may need to support more than one mode without making the experience confusing.

This is where devotional flow matters.

If every verse, hadith, or Seerah event is surrounded by too many links, popups, prompts, and recommendations, the app may technically provide more information but spiritually feel less helpful. More content does not always mean better learning.

The design goal should be controlled depth.

Users should be able to stay focused when they want focus, and expand context when they want depth.

## What Islamic app builders can learn

For developers, designers, researchers, and Islamic institutions, the paper points toward a few practical principles.

First, design around learning journeys, not only content categories. Qur'an, Hadith, and Seerah are connected in real learning, even if they are stored separately in a database.

Second, make context progressive. Start simple, then let users expand into deeper layers.

Third, make sources visible. Trust increases when users can see where content comes from and what kind of content it is.

Fourth, avoid interface clutter. Islamic learning apps should not copy every engagement pattern from social media or productivity apps.

Fifth, support different intensities of learning. Quick reflection, daily reading, structured study, and deeper research are not the same use case.

Sixth, involve real users. Good Islamic technology cannot be built only from assumptions about what Muslims need. It requires listening carefully to how people actually learn, struggle, search, and reflect.

## Why this connects to Islamic Computing

This paper fits into a wider direction I have been calling **Islamic Computing**: the use of computing, design, data, AI, and research to build tools that support Muslims in learning, worship, character, and service.

Islamic Computing is not only about digitising Islamic content. It is about asking how technology should be shaped by the nature of the knowledge it carries.

If the knowledge is sacred, the design must be careful.

If users are learning across Qur'an, Hadith, and Seerah, the system should support connection without confusion.

If people rely on the app for religious understanding, then trust, provenance, humility, and scholarly seriousness need to be built into the product from the beginning.

## In summary

The preprint argues that Islamic learning tools should support connected movement across Qur'an, Hadith, and Seerah while preserving trust, optionality, and devotional flow.

The future of Islamic apps should not only be more content or more features. It should be better learning architecture: clearer sources, layered context, respectful interaction, and design that helps Muslims understand and practise Islam with confidence.
